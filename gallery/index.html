ECOGEN is the receptacle of the diffuse-interface method (DIM), a theory that started in the late 1990s. It summarizes more than 20 years of research on the modelling of multiphase flows (involving pure phases, but also mixtures of phases), with the goal of developing mathematical models and their associated numerical methods.

What is a diffuse interface? In DIM theory, the interfaces between pure phases are captured as diffuse numerical regions: one goes continuously from one phase to the other.

This is possible thanks to thermodynamic consistency. The flow solution then no longer requires interface-tracking algorithms: it becomes easy to simulate complex topological changes between miscible or non-miscible fluids. Moreover, pressure waves (shock waves, acoustic waves) propagate and interact properly in the whole flow.

The base research on DIM is now mature enough to propose ECOGEN, a numerical tool widely usable to solve industrial as well as research multiphase-flow problems. Some recent applications involving different physical effects are presented below.

Physics of shock

Shock waves can propagate in matter whatever its physical state: gaseous, liquid or even solid. They can travel through interfaces or into a multiphase mixture of materials. Because ECOGEN is devoted to compressible, hyperbolic systems of equations, shock dynamics is included whatever the problem is.

Shock-wave propagation in ducts is a challenging problem for safety issues. Such studies are performed to improve the safety of confined installations thanks to a better knowledge of the pressure-wave characteristics. In this movie, the shock wave propagates in a Y-shaped bifurcation channel: because of the bifurcation, the amplitude of the incident pressure jump is expected to decrease in the secondary ducts.
The bubble contains a lighter fluid (helium) than the surrounding gas (air). Richtmyer-Meshkov instabilities deform the interface, where the pressure and density gradients generate vorticity. The main parameters of the flow pattern are the acoustic impedances, and so indirectly the speeds of sound, of both fluids.

Cavitation

The ability to treat cavitation problems is definitely one of the major assets of the diffuse-interface formulation. Thanks to consistent thermodynamics, it is possible to intrinsically simulate the appearance and disappearance of interfaces between a liquid and its vapour, without any specific interface-tracking algorithm.

Collapse of an ellipsoidal microbubble attached to a flat rigid surface, for the study of minimally invasive treatments of urinary stones. The simulation is 2D axisymmetric. The left panel shows the pressure in colour, overlaid by the gas volume fraction (upper half) and the numerical schlieren of mixture-density gradients (lower half). The right panel shows the maximum pressure at the rigid wall.
This very complex flow shows two major phenomena due to a high-speed bubble of water impacting a wall (on the left). A right-moving shock wave is created, and one can observe the typical interaction of a bubble containing a heavy fluid (water) in a lighter fluid (air), with Richtmyer-Meshkov instabilities. The impact on the wall also creates left-moving expansion waves, leading to the appearance of cavitation pockets due to the decreasing pressure.
In a rocket-engine turbopump, the very high speed of the blades can lead to the formation of vapour structures. In some critical cases, cavitation can then decrease the overall performance of the pump. In this picture, the cavitation pockets appearing at the leading-edge tips are captured with a thermodynamically consistent phase-change approach.

Droplet fragmentation

The simulation of droplet fragmentation is of prime interest for various industrial applications as well as for academic knowledge. The deformation of the droplet is a competition between surface tension and inertial forces (measured by the Weber number) that can lead to the creation of smaller droplets. Surface tension then determines the resulting droplet sizes.

Atomization of a water droplet by the high-speed flow produced by a shock wave at Mach 1.3. Contours of water volume fraction equal to 10⁻³, coloured by the mixture-velocity norm (dark red: high; dark blue: low), show the filaments and the potential smaller droplets.
A liquid droplet moves in a box with perfect solid walls. At the impact on the upper wall, the droplet slides, deforms, then divides into smaller droplets. Due to gravity, some small droplets fall down while a droplet slides along the vertical wall. One can observe the rebounds of the droplets as well as droplet/droplet interactions leading to coalescence.
